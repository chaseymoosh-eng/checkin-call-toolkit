# Check-in Calls: Volunteer Training Guide

*A friendly guide for volunteers who make regular check-in phone calls. Read it before your first call, and keep the quick-reference card (last page) next to the phone.*

## 1. Welcome, and thank you

Thank you for giving your time. A short, warm phone call can be the highlight of someone's week. You don't need special qualifications to do this well. What matters most is that you are **kind, curious, reliable, and a good listener**.

This guide covers:

## 2. What this program is, and what it isn't

**It is:**

- Regular, friendly phone calls with someone who has said they'd like more contact.
- A chance for them to talk about whatever *they* want to talk about, with someone who is genuinely interested.
- Usually a set period (often 4 to 8 weeks), on days and times that suit them.
- Run by a coordinator who supports you and handles anything beyond a friendly chat.

**It is not:**

- **Therapy, counselling, or medical advice.** You are a friendly voice, not a professional.
- **A crisis or emergency service.** If someone is in danger, we follow the crisis protocol (Section 8) and involve emergency services or crisis lines.
- Practical help with money, legal matters, or care. If someone needs that, you pass it to the coordinator, who can point them to the right service.
- A lifelong commitment. The calls have a clear start and end, and we prepare people for the end from the beginning.

> **Plain promise to participants:** "We're not counsellors. We're people from the community who'd like to give you a call and have a chat."

---

## 3. Why we think this helps

Loneliness is common. The World Health Organization's Commission on Social Connection estimates that about **1 in 6 people worldwide** are lonely, and links loneliness to an estimated **871,000 deaths a year** (WHO, 2025). Lonely people are about **twice as likely to become depressed** (WHO, 2025).

The model for these calls comes from a randomized trial in Texas, USA, during 2020 (Kahlon et al., 2021):

- **240 adults** receiving Meals on Wheels services (mostly older and homebound; all had at least one long-term health condition) were randomly given either phone calls or no calls.
- **16 lay callers, aged 17 to 23,** had brief training (about an hour, by video) in **empathetic listening** and in drawing out conversation. Each caller phoned 6 to 9 people.
- Calls ran for **4 weeks**: daily for the first 5 days, then the person chose how often (at least twice a week). The **person chose the times**, and calls averaged about 10 minutes.
- After 4 weeks, the people who got calls were **less lonely, less depressed and less anxious** than those who didn't. On the 3-item UCLA Loneliness Scale they scored about 1.1 points lower (a moderate effect).

Some honest limits: that trial lasted only 4 weeks, so we don't know how long the benefit lasts. Across the wider research, loneliness programs help, but the effects are usually small to moderate (Lasgaard et al., 2026, a review of 280 studies). We think regular, warm, reliable contact is worth offering, and we measure how it goes.

**What this means for you:** the key ingredients are simple. Be **reliable**, **listen with empathy**, and **let the person lead**. You don't have to fix anything.

## 4. Your role and its boundaries

### Your role

- Call at the agreed time. If you can't, let the coordinator know as early as you can.
- Listen, show interest, and let the person steer the conversation.
- Log each call briefly in the app (or tell your coordinator, if they log calls for you).
- Notice if something seems wrong, and follow the steps in Section 8.

### Boundaries that keep everyone safe

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the phone number the program gives you (or withhold your number if that's the agreed approach) | Give out your home address or personal social media |
| Keep calls to the agreed schedule | Promise extra calls or visits you haven't agreed with the coordinator |
| Pass practical requests to the coordinator | Lend or give money, buy things for them, or handle their finances |
| Say "I'm not able to do that, but I'll let our coordinator know" | Give medical, legal, or financial advice |
| Keep what they tell you confidential, within the program | Promise to keep everything secret (see Section 8) |
| Be yourself and share a little about your day if it helps | Make the call about you, or share your own problems at length |
| End calls kindly when time's up | Visit in person, unless your program has a separate, approved process for that |

If you're unsure whether something is OK, **ask the coordinator**. That is always the right answer.

## 5. How to do a call

A typical call is **10 to 20 minutes**. Shorter is fine. Some people love a long chat; others prefer a quick hello. Follow their lead, within the time you've agreed.

### Before the call

- Find a quiet place where you won't be overheard.
- Glance at your notes from last time: what did they mention? A grandchild's visit? A doctor's appointment? A favourite TV show?
- Take a breath. Your only job is to be good company.

### Step 1: Opening

- Greet them warmly and say who you are: *"Hi Margaret, it's Sam from the Friendly Calls group. Is now still a good time?"*
- Check it's still convenient. If not, agree another time and log it as rescheduled.

### Step 2: Listen with empathy

Empathetic listening means trying to understand how things feel for them, and letting them know you've understood.

- **Give your full attention.** No multitasking, no screens.
- **Use small encouragers:** "Mm-hm," "Really?", "Go on…", "Oh, that's lovely."
- **Notice feelings**, not just facts: *"That sounds like it was a really long week."*
- **Allow pauses.** A few seconds of quiet is fine. People often say the important thing after a pause.

### Step 3: Let them lead

In the research, callers let the person guide the conversation. That means:

- Follow the topics *they* bring up, rather than working through your own list.
- If they want to talk about the weather for ten minutes, that's a good call.
- If they want to talk about something hard, you can listen without trying to fix it.

### Step 4: Ask open questions

Open questions invite more than a yes or no.

| Instead of… (closed) | Try… (open) |
|---|---|
| "Did you have a good week?" | "What's your week been like?" |
| "Did you go out?" | "What have you been up to since we last spoke?" |
| "Is your garden nice?" | "Tell me about your garden. What's growing at the moment?" |
| "Are you OK?" | "How are you feeling about things today?" |

Good conversation openers: *"What was the best bit of your week?"*, *"What did you used to do for work?"*, *"What's something you're looking forward to?"*, *"Last time you mentioned… how did that go?"*

### Step 5: Reflect back

Reflecting shows you're really listening. Put what you heard into your own words:

- *"So it sounds like you really miss having people around at mealtimes."*
- *"You're feeling proud of your grandson, and a bit sad he lives so far away."*
- *"What I'm hearing is that the hospital visit was more tiring than you expected."*

If you get it slightly wrong, that's fine. They'll correct you, and the conversation keeps going.

### Step 6: Closing

- Give a gentle signal a minute or two before the end: *"I'll need to go in a few minutes, but before I do…"*
- Mention something you enjoyed: *"I loved hearing about your trip to the coast."*
- End warmly: *"It's been really nice talking to you."*

### Step 7: Schedule the next call

- Confirm the next call: *"Shall I call you again on Thursday at about half past ten?"*
- If they want to change the day or time, say yes if you can, or tell them you'll check with the coordinator.
- Remind them of the number of calls left as the end approaches (see the final-call script).

### After the call

Log it in the app within the same day: outcome, rough length, a mood check (how they seemed, 1 to 5), a line or two of notes, and whether there's any concern. Keep notes **short, factual and kind**. Write only what you'd be comfortable with them reading.

## 6. Sample call scripts

These are guides, not things to read word for word. Use your own natural words.

### First call

> "Hello, is that Rosa? Hi Rosa, my name's Priya. I'm a volunteer with the Friendly Calls group. I think [coordinator] mentioned I'd be calling. Is now still a good time for a chat?
>
> Lovely. So, just so you know how this works: I'll give you a call [twice a week] for the next [four] weeks, at a time that suits you. It's just a chance to chat about whatever you like. I'm not a counsellor or anything like that, just someone who enjoys a good conversation.
>
> One thing I should mention: what you tell me stays within our small team. The only time I'd need to share something is if I was worried about your safety, and I'd try to talk to you about it first.
>
> So, tell me a bit about yourself. How long have you lived in [area]?
>
> *(…listen, follow their lead, reflect…)*
>
> I've really enjoyed this. I'd love to call again. Would [Wednesday at 2] still work for you? Great. Is there anything that would make these calls better for you, like a better time of day, or if I should call a bit louder? Lovely. Take care, Rosa, and speak to you on Wednesday."

### Regular call

> "Hi Ade, it's Sam. How are you doing today?
>
> Last time you mentioned the match on Saturday. How did it go?
>
> *(…listen…)* You sound really pleased about that!
>
> What else has been happening this week?
>
> *(…listen, open questions, reflect…)* It sounds like the new flat is starting to feel more like home.
>
> I'll have to go in a minute or two, but before I do, is there anything else on your mind?
>
> Great talking to you as always. Same time on Friday? Brilliant. Take care."

### If they don't answer

- Let it ring for a good while (some people take time to reach the phone).
- If your program allows voicemail: *"Hi, it's Sam from Friendly Calls, just calling for our chat. No need to call back. I'll try again [time]. Take care."*
- Log it as "No answer."
- **If you can't reach them for two scheduled calls in a row, tell the coordinator.** If you were already worried about them, tell the coordinator straight away.

### Final call

Prepare for the end from about two calls before: *"Just a reminder, we've got two more calls together before the program finishes."*

> "Hi Margaret, it's Sam. So, this is our last call in this program. I've really enjoyed our chats, especially hearing about your garden and your grandchildren.
>
> How have you found the calls?
>
> *(…listen…)*
>
> [Coordinator] will be in touch about what's next. That might include [other local groups, a library club, or another round of calls, depending on what your program offers]. Is there anything you'd like me to pass on to them?
>
> *(If the program uses the short loneliness questions, the coordinator or you may ask them now.)*
>
> Thank you for letting me call. Take good care of yourself, Margaret."

**Don't** promise to stay in touch privately. If they ask, say: *"I'd love that, but I'm not allowed to under the program rules. I'll make sure [coordinator] knows you'd like more calls."*

## 7. What to avoid

| Avoid | Why | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Giving advice** ("You should…", "Have you tried…?") | It can feel like you're not listening, and it may be wrong for them | Reflect and ask: *"What do you think might help?"* |
| **Promising secrecy** ("I won't tell anyone") | You may need to share a safety concern | *"This stays within our small team, unless I'm worried about your safety."* |
| **Judging** ("You shouldn't feel like that") | It shuts people down | *"It makes sense that you'd feel that way."* |
| **Fixing or rushing to cheer up** ("Look on the bright side!") | It can make people feel unheard | Stay with the feeling for a moment: *"That sounds really hard."* |
| **Interrogating** (lots of questions in a row) | It feels like a form, not a chat | One question, then really listen |
| **Talking about yourself too much** | The call is for them | Share a little if it helps the conversation flow, then turn it back |
| **Politics, religion, money disputes** (unless they raise it and it stays friendly) | Can cause upset | Gently steer: *"Let's not get into that one! Tell me about…"* |
| **Making promises you can't keep** | Breaks trust | *"I'm not sure, but I'll ask the coordinator."* |

## 8. Warning signs, and what to do if you're worried

Most calls are simply pleasant. But sometimes you may hear something that worries you. **You are not expected to handle it alone.** Your job is to stay calm, keep them as safe as you can in the moment, and pass it on.

### Warning signs to notice

**Possible risk of suicide or self-harm** (especially if new or getting worse):

- Talking about wanting to die, or not wanting to be here
- Saying they feel hopeless, trapped, or like a burden on others
- Saying goodbye, giving things away, or "putting affairs in order" in a way that worries you
- Talking about a plan, or having the means (pills, weapons)
- Drinking or using drugs more, withdrawing from everyone, or big mood swings

**Other concerns:**

- A fall, injury, or sudden illness; confusion that's new or much worse
- Not eating, no heating, no medication, or unable to look after themselves
- Signs that someone may be harming, neglecting, scaring or taking money from them
- Repeatedly not answering when they usually do

### The crisis protocol

**1. Immediate danger to life** (they've taken something, are seriously injured, are about to harm themselves or someone else, or someone is harming them right now):

- **Call emergency services now** (for example 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 112 across the EU), or ask someone near you to call while you stay on the line.
- Give their name, address and what's happening. Stay on the phone with them if you can.
- Then **tell the coordinator immediately.**

**2. They talk about suicidal thoughts, but are not in immediate danger:**

- **Stay calm and stay with them.** Keep your voice gentle.
- **It's OK to ask directly.** Crisis services advise being direct: *"Are you thinking about ending your life?"* (988 Lifeline). Asking shows you care and that it's OK to talk.
- **Listen without judging.** Don't argue, lecture, or act shocked.
- **Help them connect to a crisis line** now: encourage them to call, or offer to help make the call. Examples: **988** in the US (call or text), **Samaritans on 116 123** in the UK and Ireland. For other countries, see **findahelpline.com**. Your coordinator will add local numbers to the app and to the card at the end of this guide.
- **Don't promise to keep it secret.** *"I care about you, and I need to let [coordinator] know so we can make sure you get support."*
- **Tell the coordinator immediately after the call**, even if they seem calmer.
- Mark the call as **Urgent** in the app; the crisis protocol and numbers will appear on screen.

**3. A non-urgent concern** (they seem lower than usual, mention money worries, need practical help, missed two calls in a row, something just doesn't feel right):

- **Log it as "Follow-up needed"** with a short, factual note.
- **Make sure the coordinator knows within 24 hours.**

> **If in doubt, treat it as more serious, and tell the coordinator.** You will never be in trouble for raising a concern.

The 988 Lifeline describes five action steps for helping someone who may be at risk: **Ask, Be there, Help keep them safe, Help them connect, Follow up.** Your part is the first four, in the moment; the coordinator leads the follow-up.

### After a difficult call

Write down what was said and what you did, with times, while it's fresh. Stick to facts. Then talk to the coordinator: not just to report it, but so *you* are supported too (see Section 10).

## 9. Confidentiality and data

- **What they tell you stays within the program team** (you and the coordinator). Don't share names or stories with friends or family, or online, even without names if they could be recognised.
- **The exception is safety.** If you're worried someone is at risk of harm, you must share that with the coordinator (and emergency services if needed). Explain this kindly at the first call.
- **Keep records brief and factual.** Write as if the person might read it.
- **Protect devices.** If you use the app or keep notes, use a device with a password or PIN, lock it when you step away, and don't leave notes where others can see them. The app stores data only on the device it's used on.
- **Never record calls.**
- **When you leave the program**, hand back or delete any notes and participant contact details, as the coordinator asks.

---

## 10. Looking after yourself

Listening is rewarding, and sometimes it's heavy. Looking after yourself is part of the role.

- **It's not your job to fix things.** Being there is enough.
- **You can't make someone less lonely on your own.** You're one part of their week.
- **Debrief.** After a hard call, talk to the coordinator. You can share the feeling without sharing details outside the team.
- **Notice your own signs:** dreading calls, thinking about a participant a lot, trouble sleeping. Tell the coordinator; it's OK to take a break or reduce your calls.
- **Keep to your hours.** Don't take on more than you agreed.
- **If a call brings up something painful for you**, you can use the same crisis lines too. They're for anyone.
